Heating

In Methods of Heating you met the particle model, temperature, internal energy and the three ways thermal energy can be transferred. This workbook puts all of that to work on a single, central problem: what happens, quantitatively, as you heat 1 kg of ice at −5 °C all the way through to steam at 120 °C? A single temperature–energy graph runs through every section of this workbook — you will keep coming back to it as you build up the tools (specific heat capacity and specific latent heat) needed to calculate exactly how much energy each stage requires.

By the end of this workbook you should be able to:
  • Recall that a phase change is a change in particle behaviour arising from a change in energy, at constant temperature.
  • Interpret a temperature–energy graph for a substance heated from solid through to gas, identifying each stage.
  • Explain how internal energy changes during warming and during a phase change, and relate this to the gradient of the graph.
  • Calculate the thermal energy transferred to a substance using Q = mcΔT, where c is the specific heat capacity.
  • Calculate the thermal energy transferred during a phase change using Q = mL, where L is the specific latent heat.
  • Combine Q = mcΔT and Q = mL, and use conservation of energy, to solve multi-stage heating and calorimetry problems.

1. The heating curve

In Methods of Heating you saw that a phase change — melting, freezing, boiling or condensing — is a change in particle behaviour that happens at constant temperature, because the energy supplied is being used to change the particles' arrangement and separation, not their average kinetic energy.

Recap. A phase change represents a change in particle behaviour arising from a change in a substance's energy, at constant temperature.

This idea becomes far more useful once you can track it on a graph. Fig. 1.1 below shows what happens as exactly 1 kg of ice, starting at −5 °C, is heated steadily all the way to steam at 120 °C. This single graph is the backbone of the whole workbook — every later section comes back to it.

1For each of the labelled regions (a)–(e) on Fig. 1.1, state whether the 1 kg sample is a solid, a liquid, a gas, or undergoing a phase change.
(a) solid (ice warming). (b) phase change — melting, solid → liquid. (c) liquid (water warming). (d) phase change — boiling, liquid → gas. (e) gas (steam warming).
2Explain why the temperature does not change during regions (b) and (d), even though energy is still being transferred to the substance the whole time.
During a phase change the energy supplied is used to change the arrangement and separation of the particles (weakening or breaking the forces between them), not to increase their average kinetic energy. Since temperature is a measure of average particle kinetic energy, the temperature stays constant until the phase change is complete.
3Describe how the arrangement and motion of the particles differs between region (a) and region (c).
In region (a) the substance is solid ice: particles are held close together in a regular, touching arrangement and can only vibrate about fixed positions. In region (c) it is liquid water: particles are still close together and touching, but the arrangement is disordered and particles are free to move past one another, so the liquid can flow.

2. Internal energy along the curve

Recall that the internal energy of a substance is the sum of the total random kinetic energy and total potential energy of all its particles. Every joule of energy supplied in Fig. 1.1 increases the internal energy of the sample — the graph simply shows you which form that increase takes at each stage.

Key idea. Where the temperature is changing — (a), (c) or (e) — the internal energy change is a change in the kinetic energy: the particles move (or vibrate) faster/slower, on average, and the temperature changes.
Key idea. During a phase-change region — (b) or (d) — the internal energy changes as the bonding between particles changes while their average kinetic energy — and so their temperature — stays the same. This is a change in potential energy.

The gradient of the graph

The steepness of each warming ramp is not the same. Region (a) is much steeper than region (c): a small amount of energy raises the temperature of ice by a lot, while the same amount of energy raises the temperature of water by much less.

Worth remembering Gradient and specific heat capacity. For a warming region, the gradient of a temperature–energy graph is ΔT/ΔQ. As you will see in Section 3, ΔQ = mcΔT, so the gradient is equal to 1/(mc). A substance with a small specific heat capacity c (like ice) produces a steep gradient; a substance with a large c (like water) produces a shallow one.
4State which form of energy (kinetic or potential) is increasing during region (c), and which is increasing during region (d).
Region (c): kinetic energy is increasing (the liquid water is warming). Region (d): potential energy is increasing (the water is boiling — particles are being separated into a gas against the forces between them, at constant temperature).
5Region (a) is much steeper than region (c) on Fig. 1.1. Suggest a reason for this, in terms of the specific heat capacities of ice and water.
The specific heat capacity of ice is smaller than that of water, so a given amount of energy produces a larger temperature change in ice than in the same mass of water. Since the gradient of the graph is 1/(mc), a smaller c gives a steeper gradient — this is why region (a) rises so much more sharply than region (c).
6Explain, in terms of internal energy, why melting ice at 0 °C requires a continuous supply of energy even though the temperature does not change.
The energy supplied during melting increases the potential energy of the particles as the fixed, ordered solid structure is broken down into the more loosely-arranged liquid structure. This raises the total internal energy of the substance even though the average kinetic energy of the particles — and hence the temperature — does not change.

3. Specific heat capacity

Section 2 showed that the gradient of a warming region depends on the substance. To calculate warming energies precisely, physicists use the specific heat capacity, c, of a substance.

7Why is the x-axis of Fig. 3.1 labelled "Time," when the equivalent graph in Section 1 (Fig. 1.1) was labelled "Energy supplied"?
If the substance is heated by a heater working at constant power P, the energy supplied is directly proportional to the time for which it has been heating (Q = Pt). This means a graph of temperature against time has exactly the same shape as a graph of temperature against energy supplied — only the horizontal axis label (and its scale) changes. The same graph can be used for either, provided the heating power stays constant.
Specific heat capacity, c: the amount of thermal energy needed to raise the temperature of a unit mass (1 kg) of a substance by 1 K (or 1 °C — a change of 1 K is identical to a change of 1 °C).

Regions (a), (c) and (e) on Fig. 1.1 — the three warming ramps — are all governed by specific heat capacity. The thermal energy Q transferred is given by:

Worked example A 0.20 kg aluminium block is heated from 18 °C to 85 °C. Calculate the thermal energy required. (caluminium = 897 J kg−1 K−1)

ΔT = 85 − 18 = 67 K
Q = mcΔT
Q = 0.20 × 897 × 67

80.50 kg of water is heated by 30,000 J. Calculate the temperature rise, ΔT. (cwater = 4180 J kg−1 K−1)
Rearranging Q = mcΔT for ΔT:
ΔT = Q/(mc)
ΔT = 30 000/(0.50 × 4180)
9A 0.30 kg sample of an unknown liquid absorbs 8100 J of energy and its temperature rises from 20 °C to 41 °C. Calculate the specific heat capacity of the liquid.
ΔT = 41 − 20 = 21 K
Rearranging Q = mcΔT for c:
c = Q/(mΔT)
c = 8100/(0.30 × 21)
10Equal masses of copper and water, both initially at the same temperature, are given equal amounts of thermal energy. Explain which one reaches a higher final temperature, and why.
Copper reaches the higher final temperature. Copper has a much smaller specific heat capacity than water (390 compared with 4180 J kg⁻¹K⁻¹), so for the same mass and the same energy input, ΔT = Q/(mc) is much larger for copper than for water.

4. Specific latent heat

Regions (b) and (d) on Fig. 1.1 — the two plateaus — cannot be calculated with Q = mcΔT, because ΔT = 0 during a phase change. Instead, physicists use the specific latent heat, L, of a substance.

Summative task — Slide 6 (continued). Identify the areas on the graph described by specific latent heat, and explain the term.
Specific latent heat, L: the amount of thermal energy needed to change the state of a unit mass (1 kg) of a substance, without any change in temperature.

The thermal energy Q transferred during a phase change is given by:

11Calculate the thermal energy needed to melt 0.75 kg of ice at 0 °C. (Lf = 334 000 J kg−1)
Q = mLf
Q = 0.75 × 334 000
12A 0.20 kg sample of a substance requires 45 000 J of energy to completely melt at its melting point. Calculate its specific latent heat of fusion.
Rearranging Q = mL for L:
L = Q/m
L = 45 000/0.20
13Explain, in terms of particle separation, why the specific latent heat of vaporisation of water is so much larger than its specific latent heat of fusion.
During melting, particles remain close together — they gain enough energy to move past one another, but the forces between them are still largely intact. During vaporisation, particles must be separated almost completely, moving from close together (liquid) to far apart (gas) against the full strength of the attractive forces between them. Much more energy is needed to achieve this much larger increase in separation, so Lv ≫ Lf.
14Combined question. Calculate the total thermal energy needed to convert 0.50 kg of ice at 0 °C into water at 40 °C. (Lf = 334 000 J kg−1; cwater = 4180 J kg−1K−1)
This is a two-stage process: melting the ice, then warming the resulting water.
Stage 1 (melt): Q1 = mLf = 0.50 × 334 000
Stage 2 (warm): Q2 = mcΔT = 0.50 × 4180 × 40
Total energy Q = Q1 + Q2 — calculate each stage, then add them together.

Conservation of energy: calorimetry

When two substances at different temperatures are combined in an isolated system, the total energy is conserved: the thermal energy lost by the hotter substance equals the thermal energy gained by the cooler one.

Key idea. For an isolated system: energy lost by the hotter substance = energy gained by the cooler substance.
19A 0.10 kg block of iron at 150 °C is dropped into 0.40 kg of water at 15 °C, in an insulated container. Assuming no energy is lost to the surroundings, set up (but do not solve) the conservation of energy equation for this system, and identify what it could be used to find. (ciron = 450 J kg−1K−1; cwater = 4180 J kg−1K−1)
Energy lost by iron = energy gained by water:
mironciron(150 − Tf) = mwatercwater(Tf − 15)
0.10 × 450 × (150 − Tf) = 0.40 × 4180 × (Tf − 15)
This equation could be solved for Tf, the final equilibrium temperature of the iron and water once they reach thermal equilibrium.

Glossary

Phase change
A change in particle behaviour (arrangement and separation) arising from a change in a substance's energy, at constant temperature — e.g. melting, boiling.
Internal energy
The sum of the total random kinetic energy and total potential energy of all the particles in a substance.
Specific heat capacity, c
The thermal energy needed to raise the temperature of a unit mass (1 kg) of a substance by 1 K; Q = mcΔT.
Specific latent heat, L
The thermal energy needed to change the state of a unit mass (1 kg) of a substance, at constant temperature; Q = mL.
Specific latent heat of fusion, Lf
The specific latent heat associated with melting or freezing.
Specific latent heat of vaporisation, Lv
The specific latent heat associated with boiling or condensing; always much larger than Lf for the same substance.
Boiling
A phase change from liquid to gas occurring throughout the bulk of a liquid, only at the boiling point, at constant temperature.
Evaporation
A phase change from liquid to gas occurring only at the free surface of a liquid, at any temperature, as the most energetic particles escape; produces a cooling effect on the liquid left behind.
Conservation of energy (calorimetry)
In an isolated system, the total energy is conserved: thermal energy lost by a hotter substance equals thermal energy gained by a cooler substance.
